A few weeks ago I shared the news with my team - I was leaving the company. As an engineering leader, these moments are always full of tensions: how will my team take it? Will they be ok?

This isn’t the first company I’ve had to say goodbye to in my career, so there are certain truths I have come to know - of course everyone will be fine, and of course they’ll move on and continue to do amazing things without me. After all, doing good work at any company didn’t start with me, so why would my leaving change that?

But something did surprise me this time. My Slack DMs started to fill up with messages I didn’t expect.

*“Your courses were so helpful for me. Learning how to write queries unlocked so many things.”*

*“I love that you can impart truth and keep things rational despite the loopy nature of our work.”*

*“Been meaning to reach out.”*

So many people I worked with, some closely, and many that I was a lot less close with, took the time to tell me about a standout moment they remembered, or something they learned from working with me. I considered it a great privilege in my time at Wayfair to work alongside a long list of talented individuals, many of whom had a profound impact on me; but what I didn’t realize was all the ways I might’ve made a difference to them.

These messages were truly humbling, but made me reflect on these moments - why is it so common that we wait for someone to depart before we say these things out loud?

![Assumption](./section1.jpg)

## We assume they already know

This might be the first and most important one of all, especially in the role of an engineering leader. At work, we are all really quick to let someone know when they make a mistake or need to correct something. After all, not doing that gives someone more chances to repeat the mistake, or not improve. That often means many of us fall into the trap of “no news is good news,” or simply: if you were doing something wrong, someone would have told you, so silence must mean everything is ok.

I heard a saying long ago at one of my earliest jobs: “It takes 10 atta-boys to make up for one oh-shoot” (the actual saying was slightly more colorful). This is a crucial thing for leaders to remember. Everyone remembers the bad stuff because we don’t hear the good stuff nearly often enough. So let’s not be shy with it!

The same silence that lets us assume “no news is good news” about our own work leads us to assume our appreciation is obvious to everyone else. We figure our peers already know that we loved working with them, or that we valued their impact on us. But I’d challenge that. Do any of us really know how everyone around us feels? Maybe a kind note, a smile, or a genuine thank-you would make all the difference in the world!

![Urgency](./section2.jpg)

## Urgency over importance

It’s a simple truth - we’re all so busy most days. Just at work, most of us are juggling full calendars, endless task lists full of things we need to do (that just always seem to pile up), and impromptu meetings or huddles to quickly knock out that really tricky thing we just couldn’t get past. Of course we’re not stopping to reflect on the impact others have on us, or whether we’re impacting them.

More often than not, once the moment has passed, we think it’s less critical to tell them about. After all, it probably happened a week (or month, or year) ago, it probably doesn’t even matter now.

I’m willing to bet that gratitude isn’t even on your to-do list as you read this, is it?

![Vulnerable](./section3.jpg)

## It can feel vulnerable

Speculative though this one may be, I suspect it’s still quite true - telling someone you work with how their work impacted you can feel quite emotionally vulnerable. After all, we’re all “professionals”, aren’t we? Do we really need to make everyone around us “feel good”?

I totally get this one - it probably feels really weird to have a heart to heart with someone, or just tell them how much you loved something they did. Amazing presentation? Super helpful document they wrote? A bug fixed, feature shipped, a witty insight, tribal knowledge nugget - all of these things carry weight, and yet we’re often wary of a simple gesture of thanks.

![Reflection](./section4.jpg)

## Endings make us reflect

Most often, we just aren’t really introspecting about the impact others have on us until there’s a reason to bring those thoughts out. This is natural - reflection isn’t usually something we do proactively. We rarely stop and ponder the connection we have with someone we work with every day, so an ending is a natural forcing function.

Not only that, it’s also a moment that practically asks for it. After all, very soon that colleague isn’t going to be just there on Slack (or whatever chat program your work uses), so the natural reaction is to give it some thought while they’re still a message away.

I think this last point is the real thing that sticks out for me - it’s not that anyone is deliberately withholding anything, nor that we didn’t have permission. We just didn’t pause long enough to notice it - until something made us stop.

Something that stuck with me was that none of these messages were about big things in most cases, at least, not from my point of view. Often they referenced a small conversation over coffee, a tiny pointer or bit of feedback, or a small moment of perspective. I say this in the nicest possible way, but I honestly probably don’t even remember the exact conversation in most cases, and maybe, neither do they.

But they recalled how it made them feel.

Here’s a thought to leave you with, and maybe for myself as well. Try, even just once a week, to tell someone you work with one thing they did to make your week great, or how much their work mattered. It doesn’t have to be big or grandiose - just a way to express a thank you for a moment that was special. If you can, be specific, too.

Don’t save it for the Send-Off - tell them today.
